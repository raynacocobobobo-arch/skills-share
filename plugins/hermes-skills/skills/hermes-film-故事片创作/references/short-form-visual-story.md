# Short-form Visual Story Toolkit

Use this reference for roughly 1–5 minute narrative films, three-minute fiction, image-led micro stories, low-dialogue stories, parallel montage, or any task where the user explicitly asks for more visual storytelling and less explanation.

This is a compression mode, not a separate story theory. The main `故事片创作` skill still controls source priority, revision scope, causality, character choice, realism, and dialogue/VO rules.

## 1. Do not compress a long film into three minutes

A short-form story should usually begin inside an already charged situation rather than spending its opening on world setup.

Prefer:
- one dominant dramatic question;
- one main relationship or conflict line;
- one decisive change or choice;
- implication of a larger world instead of explanation of the whole world.

Avoid:
- miniaturized feature-film structure with many roles, institutions, rules, and reversals;
- front-loaded worldbuilding;
- a new plot mechanism every 15–20 seconds;
- explaining every cause that the audience can infer from visible consequences.

## 2. Information budget

Every visible detail, object, line, or sound should do more than one job when possible.

Good short-form elements can simultaneously:
- identify a person;
- establish time or distance;
- carry emotion;
- create suspense;
- prepare a later payoff;
- reveal the world without exposition.

If an element performs only decorative mood work and the story stays unchanged without it, cut or combine it.

## 3. Start with a charged image or situation

The opening should create a concrete audience question quickly.

Examples of useful question types:
- Why is the expected person absent?
- Why is everyone behaving normally around an abnormal situation?
- What is this person waiting for?
- Why is this object being protected or delivered?
- What happens when the visible countdown/condition reaches its end?

Do not answer the opening question immediately. Let actions progressively redefine it.

## 4. Show the rule only as far as the choice requires

Short-form science fiction and professional settings often collapse under explanation.

Show enough for the audience to understand:
- what is wrong;
- who can act;
- what each available action causes;
- why the final action cannot simply be undone.

Do not explain the whole machine, institution, history, or procedure unless that information changes the decision.

A useful test:
> Can the audience understand the choice from spatial relation, status lights, repeated actions, visible consequences, or a simple diagram before a character explains it?

If yes, remove the explanation.

## 5. Montage must create a third meaning

Parallel montage is not illustration.

Weak montage:
- line says “illness” → cut to a wound;
- line says “separation” → cut to two people apart.

Stronger montage:
- Track A has one literal meaning;
- Track B has another literal meaning;
- their collision creates a third emotional or narrative meaning that neither track carries alone.

For vows, announcements, broadcasts, songs, ceremonies, or institutional language, ask:
> Does the sound merely describe the picture, or does the picture change what the words mean?

Keep only the latter when possible.

## 6. Repetition and meaning shift

Short-form stories gain depth when one repeated element changes meaning across the piece.

Candidates:
- a sentence;
- a gesture;
- a light or sound;
- an object;
- a framing device;
- a routine instruction;
- a countdown or interval.

Useful pattern:
> setup → repetition → changed context → payoff

The strongest payoff is often not “the object returns,” but “the same object/line/action now means something different.”

## 7. Visual action filter: cut water actions

Every notable action should perform at least one function:
- change the situation;
- reveal or alter a relationship;
- provide information needed later;
- create or release suspense;
- set up or pay off something;
- embody a choice;
- replace explanation with visible evidence.

If none apply, cut it.

Warning cluster:
- looks;
- lowers head;
- pauses;
- sighs;
- walks a few steps;
- looks again;
- breathes heavily.

These actions are not forbidden, but they must alter interpretation or state. “Cinematic” filler is still filler.

## 8. Rhythm: compression is not constant speed

A useful short-form rhythm is:
1. strong opening image/question;
2. hold the central problem long enough to orient the audience;
3. compress repeated or procedural action;
4. accelerate near the irreversible choice;
5. after the choice, slow down sharply and let one image carry the consequence.

Do not make every second equally dense. Weight comes from contrast.

## 9. Large background must participate in the story

If the project depends on a strong world, giant architecture, city scale, landscape, institution, or environment, the background cannot remain decorative.

Make scale affect at least one of:
- what characters can see but cannot reach;
- what they can know or communicate;
- how long movement takes;
- what a small human action changes;
- how the audience understands cost or consequence.

A large world should change the drama, not merely make the frame impressive.

## 10. Short-form final pass

Before delivery, ask:
- Can the story be stated as one dominant dramatic question?
- Does the opening enter the charged situation quickly?
- Does each scene/detail earn its time?
- Are there explanations the image can replace?
- Does montage create new meaning rather than illustrate dialogue?
- Does at least one repeated element gain new meaning?
- Are there water actions that can be cut?
- Does the final image carry consequence without another paragraph of explanation?

If the answer to several is no, compress again before adding material.
